# Introduction

## The March Route

On 08 November 2026, the 52<sup>nd</sup> annual Canadian Liberation
March was held to honour the sacrifice of the Canadian Army in the
liberation of Belgium and the Netherlands during Operation Switchback.
The 33 km march follows the progress of the Canadians from Hoofdplaat,
Netherlands to Knokke-Heist Belgium.

## JFC Brunssum

We had always wondered why the Canadian Military Contingent was from
Allied Joint Forces Command (JFC) Brunssum. Teacher Rachel asked Danny
during the walk and got the story. Soon after the walk started the group
thought it would be appropriate to have Canadian Military participation
so asked the Canadian Embassy. The Embassy detailed the Canadian Base in
Lahr, Germany to march and the first year one officer and a few soldiers
participated. They had a great time and told the committee not to ask
the embassy again as they wanted to come back. They participated every
year until the base was closed in 1994. At the time, one of the former
Lahr marchers was at JFC Brunssum and volunteered their members who have
been marching ever since. The main contingent is from JFC, with a few
spots given out to other Canadians in the Area.

Similarly, the AF North Teachers started with a single representative
and the participation has increased dramatically in recent years.

Teachers Rachel, Christine and Whitney all from the class of 2027, have
done the full 33km for all four years of their tenure.

# Battle of the Scheldt

The British 30 Corps had captured the port of Antwerp on 04 September
1944 but were ordered to stop at the Albert Canal. The Germans occupied
both sides of the Scheldt Estuary, a total length of 50 km, so the
Canadians were given the task of clearing the Estuary so that Allied
shipping could deliver supplies into the port of Antwerp.

Second Division of the First Canadian Army cleared the northern bank of
the Scheldt, the Third Division the Southern bank as part of Operation
Switchback, and the Fourth Canadian Armoured Division pushed North of
Antwerp.

## Operation Switchback

Operation Switchback was a fierce and critical military offensive
conducted during World War II as part of the broader Battle of the
Scheldt. Lasting from October 6 to November 3, 1944, its primary
objective was to clear German forces from the Breskens Pocket—an area
located south of the Western Scheldt estuary, trapped between northern
Belgium and southwestern Netherlands.

Clearing this pocket was essential to opening the vital deep-water port
of Antwerp to Allied shipping, which was desperately needed to supply
the advancing armies pushing toward Germany.

The operation was spearheaded by the 3rd Canadian Infantry Division.
Historians frequently describe the Breskens Pocket as some of the worst
terrain encountered by soldiers during the war. Consisting of reclaimed
lowlands (*polders*), the area was characterized by a flat grid of
soaked agricultural fields bordered by deep drainage canals and high,
steep dikes (5 to 8 meters tall). Heavy October rains and deliberate
flooding by the Germans saturated the terrain, making movement nearly
impossible for infantry and entirely prohibitive for standard tanks.

Canadian forces suffered 2,077 total casualties (killed, wounded, and
missing) while clearing the Breskens Pocket.

## Aardenburg

Aardenburg was liberated on 19 October by the Highland Light Infantry.
The town was almost completely destroyed with only 35 of 1000 buildings
undamaged. The memorial ceremony was held at the Aardenburg Communal
Cemetery, where a Lancaster bomber crew is buried. The Lancaster ND919
from 75 (New Zealand) Squadron was part of a 110 aircraft raid on the
Leuven/ Louvain railway yards. Most of the bombing hit the railway
workshops and storage buildings rather than the yards. In the raid 4
Lancasters, including ND919, were lost. ND919 was attacked by a night
fighter and exploded in mid air. The crew consisting of 2 Canadians, 1
Australian and 4 Brits, were buried in the Aardenburg cemetery in a
communal grave.

## Sluis

Sluis was liberated on 01 November 1944 by Le Regiment de la Chaudière.
Weeks of artillery shelling, mortar fire, and air attacks struck the
town during the fighting in western Zeeuws-Vlaanderen. Many buildings
were damaged or destroyed before Canadian troops entered the area. The
historic belfry (Belfort), the only medieval belfry in the Netherlands,
was heavily damaged by the fighting in October 1944 and had to be
restored after the war, with restoration completed in 1962.

Sluis is a beautiful, quiet city today.

## Percy Mervyn

<img
src="images/liberation-march-november-2026/image_25.png"
style="width:3.96875in;height:8.4375in"
alt="A close-up of a young person AI-generated content may be incorrect." />

I have previously also written about [Percy
Mervyn](https://www.paulsbattlefieldtours.com/post/private-percy-mervyn-at-the-leopold-canal),
an uncle of Barb Drury, my oldest sister Diane’s best friend. Percy had
just turned 19 when he enlisted in January 1944. He was sent to England
in August 1944 and joined the Royal Winnipeg Rifles on 19 September. The
Royal Winnipeg Rifles had suffered 700 casualties from D-Day to the end
of September out of a 1000-man battalion, so were desperate for more
troops. Manpower shortages plagued all units.

To alleviate the shortage, rear areas were scoured for excess personnel
that could be sent to the infantry units. Cooks, typists, mechanics,
electricians, anybody could suddenly be presented with an infantrymen’s
kit and assigned to a rifle battalion. Most of these soldiers had long
forgotten even rudimentary infantry skills, so feeding them into the
front lines little bolstered a battalion’s fighting ability.

Even reinforcements drawn from the designated infantry pool were proving
poorly trained. The Black Watch war diarist reported that most of the
reinforcements it was receiving were “a good lot, with all sorts of
confidence and a truly aggressive spirit,” but were also “inexperienced
and like to dash out to see what is happening if an air burst or shell
goes off anywhere in the neighbourhood.” Many were soon “throwing
grenades and firing the PIAT for the first time.”

Percy was killed on 10 October 1944 during the crossing of the Leopold
Canal at the age of 19, three weeks after joining the regiment.

## For Freedom Museum

The final stop of the evening was at the For Freedom Museum. Danny
Lannoy leading the pipe band to the museum. In addition to an excellent
museum, they are great hosts, liberally dispensing their delicious For
Freedom Beer.

The For Freedom Museum is a private museum run by Danny and Freddy
Jones. Their father, Dennis Jones, returned after the war to marry a
local Knokke-Heist girl and began to collect artifacts of war. Danny and
Freddy initially set up temporary displays before getting access to

### Leopold Canal

The initial assault of the Leopold Canal was made by the Algonquin
Regiment of 4<sup>th</sup> Canadian Armoured Division. The Algonquins
were to cross the canal on boats and set up a bridgehead so the
remainder of 4<sup>th</sup> Armoured Division could push straight north
and capture the port of Breskens to block the German escape route.

At 0530 hours, 13 September, A and B Companies of the Algonquin
Regiment, supported by Royal Canadian Engineers, began the assault from
Moerkerke. The Algonquins launched in storm boats and Kapok assault
floats, paddling across under intense machine-gun and mortar fire from
the German 64th Infantry Division entrenched on the far bank. Within
minutes, many boats were destroyed; survivors scrambled up the steep
north bank to dig in under constant fire.

> “Men went into the water up to their necks, dragging their rifles and
> Bren guns, trying to get footing in the mud. The canal bank was like a
> wall.”

At the point the Algonquins crossed, the Canal de la Deviation de la Lys
runs parallel to the Leopold Canal, so the regiment had to drag their
boats down a steep bank, paddle across the canal, clamber up the next
steep bank, cross the middle strip, then back down and up the Leopold
Canal all under heavy fire.

<img
src="images/liberation-march-november-2026/image_35.jpeg"
style="width:6.5in;height:8.40764in"
alt="A map of a city AI-generated content may be incorrect." />

Of approximately 360 Algonquin soldiers who crossed the canal, only
about 90 returned unhurt. The regiment suffered 85 killed, 120 wounded,
and 50 captured — an estimated 70% casualty rate in three days.

Historian C.P. Stacey called the Leopold Canal crossing “one of the most
desperate small-scale actions fought by Canadian troops in Northwest
Europe.”

The next attempt went in on 06 October with the7th Brigade Regina Rifles
and Canadian Scottish Regiments attacking. They used Wasp Flamethrowers
to reach across the canal to provide a surprise for the early phase of
the battle. The flamethrowers operated for 5 minutes and had the north
shore of the canal ablaze. Unfortunately, the flames illuminated the
boats crossings, and an entire company was lost to machine gun fire.

Much of the area beyond the canal had been flooded, so the 7th Brigade
had a limited area to operate in.

<img
src="images/liberation-march-november-2026/image_36.jpeg"
style="width:6.5in;height:4.78472in" alt="ree" />

The units eventually linked up but were on a very narrow strip of ground
and subjected to constant artillery fire and repeated counterattacks.

Shelling and Small Arms fire continued to make it impossible to move
about in forward areas except by crawling on the semi flooded ground or
in water filled ditches, both of which were littered with German and
Canadian dead.

Oostburg was liberated by the Queen’s Own Rifles (QOR) on 25 and 26
October 1944. As is the case in many of the small towns, the memorial to
the Canadians is beside the memorial to the locals who lost their lives
in the conflict.

The plaques honouring the QOR Liberation of the town. As part of the
ceremony, each individual name is read out. This specific commemoration,
saying this particular soldier gave his life for the liberation of this
town, is a deeply moving reminder of the importance of remembrance.

Similar to many towns in the region, Oostburg was obliterated by Allied
bombing and shelling, with 353 buildings destroyed and over 100
civilians killed. Once the Canadians took the town, the Germans
relentlessly shelled the town in preparation for counterattacks.

There is never any mention of the destruction of the towns. In an
article for Reader’s Digest 20 years after the war, the author asked if
there were bad feelings about the destruction, the original organizer of
the march just shrugged, and said, it was war. Liberation, no matter the
cost, was preferable to living in tyranny and Oppression of the Nazis.

## Hickman Bridge

After a quick stop for lunch at Zwin college put on by the residents,
the troops moved on to Hickmanbrug in Retranchement. After the
liberation of Oostburg on 29 October 1944, the Canadian forces were left
with one last obstacle; the drainage canal near Retranchement. The
Hickman Bridge, built by the Royal Canadian Engineers, was named by the
engineers after their fallen sergeant. After the completion of the
bridge, Canadian troops were able to eliminate the last pockets of
resistance around Knokke, finally liberating all of Belgium on 2
November. Operation Switchback was thus successfully completed.

Ceremony at the Hickman Bridge memorial.

Right after Hickman Bridge, there is a stop for the Military Contingent
to have refreshments with the reenactors who have been driving the WW2
vehicles.

The military is very good at training their personnel. They are trained
to have a beer after the march, to march after having too many beers the
night before, but the gap in the training is having beer while you
march. This became evident in the home stretch of the march. Just before
entering Knokke, the route went through a large area of road
construction. Suddenly, a few members broke ranks and sprinted off to
conduct a critical inspection of the construction porta potty, making
sure it was safe for the construction crew to use. Community service
inspection complete, they doubled back to the group and followed the
band for the final ceremony.

The final rest stop for the civilians before the end is a Beer Barn and
facilities, where the slackers like me join for the final 4 km victory
lap back into Knokke-Heist. It’s fun to see all the youth groups and
collections of long-time walkers and even some Belgian service personnel
stop by for a beer before the home stretch.

Teachers are only permitted to stay for a maximum of four years, and

The ceremony ended with the singing of the Canadian, Dutch and Belgian
National Anthems. To sing your National Anthem after a weekend with so
many Canadian flags, and heartfelt expressions of gratitude from the
Dutch and Belgian is a very powerful moment.

## Canadian Military Contingent

Once again, the Canadian Military folks who participated in the weekend
did an outstanding job. Over the three days, they attended the
equivalent of 10 Remembrance Day Ceremonies with a 33 km march thrown
in, always conducting themselves with extreme professionalism,
representing the Canadian Armed Forces and Canada in an exemplary
manner.

Sunday morning on the bus on the way to the start of the march the
Commanding Officer said:

> *"Regardless of how much you trained for this, you will likely feel
> some discomfort. Keep in mind that the distance we cover in 6 hours or
> so we are walking the Canadians covered in 25 days in battlefield
> conditions. Remember why we are doing this"*

It is wonderful to see all services and ranks marching together. Their
actions make me extremely proud to be a former service member and
prouder to be a Canadian. The importance to the members is highlighted
by the fact that there is a competition to be able to participate in the
weekend.

Epilogue

Thanks to all those who shared pictures for the post and especially
Vlast Pojman, the photographer who documented the weekend for freely
sharing his excellent work.

One of the youngest marchers, at age 7, did 16 km with only the
occasional portage on her dad’s shoulders. At the end of the ceremony,
she loudly proclaimed: “I’m never going to do THAT again!” I’m betting
she’ll be back. After an emotionally and physically draining weekend,
the bus ride home was much more subdued than the ride up.

This is a fabulous event, the combination of the seriousness of the
ceremonies with the rollicking good time of the March highlights the
importance of the Canadian Contribution and the deep regard the
residents have for Canadians some 80 years on. All teachers and families
should take the opportunity to immerse themselves in the March weekend.
I would recommend to all Canadians who have the opportunity to be in
Europe to set aside the first weekend in November to participate in this
outstanding commemoration.

# History

Gdansk was founded as a fortified port in the 960s and remained part of
the Duchy/Kingdom of Poland until 1227.

<img
src="images/gdansk---the-first-shots-of-the-second-world-war/image_3.png"
style="width:6.5in;height:5.82917in" />

The Kingdom of Poland broke apart and Gdansk was under the rule of the
dukes of Pomerelia, one of the regions of the former Poland from 1227 to
1282.

The Teutonic Knights, were a German Catholic Military Order who served
in the Holy Land defending Crusader territories and protecting the
Pilgrims. They shifted focus in the late 13<sup>th</sup> century,
launching campaigns against pagans in Prussia, seizing Gdansk in 1308
and holding power until 1454. The Knights originally came to Gdansk to
help expel invaders, but refused to leave, slaughtered the inhabitants
and took over the region.

After the Thirteen Years’ War end in in 1454, Gdansk became part of
Royal Prussia within the Polish Crown. This is the city’s longest period
of prosperity, with wide autonomy, its own laws, and major Baltic trade.
From1454–1569 Gdansk was part of the Kingdom of Poland, and from
1569–1793 part of the Polish Lithuanian Commonwealth. During this period
Gdansk developed a unique architectural and cultural identity deeply
rooted in its historical connections with the Low Countries. Beginning
with the Hanseatic League (trading group) in the 14th century and
intensifying during the 16th and 17th centuries, the city became a
primary destination for Dutch and Flemish merchants, artisans, and
religious refugees. This influx was driven by trade opportunities, the
80 Years' War against the Spanish, and the migration of Mennonite
communities. At the height of this period over 2,000 Dutch and Flemish
ships yearly visited the harbour.

During this period Gdansk was acknowledged as one of the most beautiful
and wealthiest cities in Europe.

<img
src="images/gdansk---the-first-shots-of-the-second-world-war/image_0.jpeg"
style="width:6.5in;height:4.89444in" />

The Long Market, home of the wealthiest residents, clearly shows its
Flanders heritage.

<img
src="images/gdansk---the-first-shots-of-the-second-world-war/image_4.jpeg"
style="width:6.5in;height:8.63264in" />

Prussia annexed Gdansk in the Second Partition of Poland (1793). With a
short interruption, 1807-1814, as the free city of Danzig under
Napolean, Gdansk remained part of Prussia until the end of the First
World War.

As part of the Treaty of Versailles in 1919, Gdansk was declared a Free
City under the League of Nations. Poland controlled foreign policy,
customs, port access, the Polish Post Office, and a military outpost at
Westerplatte, guarding the harbour. This was a compromise agreement that
provided Poland access to the Baltic Sea but did not put the 90% German
population under Polish Rule. This gave rise to German Nationalism and
by 1936 the Nazi Party was in control. The Nazi party began to identify
enemies of the city state and Hitler pressed for annexation. Poland’s
borders were much reduced as part of this settlement and having Germany
on both sides was a recipe for disaster. Gdansk is the small green area
top right.

<img
src="images/gdansk---the-first-shots-of-the-second-world-war/image_5.png"
style="width:6.5in;height:5.39444in" />

Germany and Russia invaded Poland in September 1939 and occupied the
country. Poles and Jews were persecuted, deported, or murdered and
multiple concentration camps were established in Poland.

After the war, the Potsdam Conference returned Gdansk to Poland but
Poland was placed under Soviet control and became a Communist Country.
The German population was expelled; Poles imported and the city was
rebuilt and again became a major shipbuilding center. In the 1980s,
Gdansk became the birthplace of Solidarity, which helped end communist
rule. Since 1989, it has been part of the Republic of Poland.

# Invasion

## Treaties

Not that it did them any good, but Poland had nonaggression treaties
with both Germany (1934) and Russia (1932) after several Poland-Russia
battles in the 1920s. As Germany continued to annex countries, on 19 May
1939 France and Poland signed an agreement that France would support
Poland in the event of an invasion. France agreed to immediate Air
strikes, a limited invasion by day 3 and a full-scale invasion of
Germany by day 15. France did declare war on Germany on 03 September and
launched a small offensive but decided to go on the defensive and leave
Poland to fight on their own. The Poles were betrayed as they had built
their defensive plans based on French intervention.

On 31 Mar 1939, Britain agreed to come to Poland’s defence if they were
invaded and the countries signed a full pact of mutual assistance on 25
August 1939. A week later, Germany invaded Poland and Britian declared
war two days later, much to the surprise of Hitler, who assumed the
Brits would do nothing as they had in previous annexations.

The secret treaty between Germany and Russia, the Molotov–Ribbentrop
Pact (23 August 1939) carved up Poland, the Baltic States, Finland, and
Romania between Germany and the USSR. This was a great surprise to
Britain who had counted on Russia as an ally to blunt German Aggression.

# Nazi Rise to Power

Since Gdansk had been under Prussian rule since 1793, it was about 90%
German and as the Nazis rose to power the push for Annexation became
stronger. By 1936 the Nazi party was in power in Gdansk and began to
build their list of enemies, mostly activists and Jews to be rounded up
as soon as war began. Over 20,000 prisoners were executed in the first
two months after the invasion. The building that is now the New Town
Hall was built as a Prussian Garrison in 1901, taken over by the league
of Nations in 1919, then served as German Headquarters after the fall of
Gdansk. After the war it was taken over by the Communists, becoming a
cultural centre in 1991 after the fall of communism before finally
becoming the New City Hall in 1999.

## German Invasion

### Westerplatte – 01 September 1939

Germany started massing troops at the border and creating several false
flag operations to provide an excuse for their invasion. The German ship

marines hidden on board attacked. They expected a walk over but instead

### Gdansk Post Office

The Post Office was one of the few Polish controlled entities in Gdansk
so they were militarily trained and armed with machine guns. At 0430 on
01 September, Danzig police, *Schutzstaffel* (SS) troops – Hitler’s
police force and *Sturm Abeilung* (SA)- Brownshirts units attack the
Polish Post Office with armoured cars, artillery, and over 180 men. The
defenders included 56 people — postal workers, volunteers, and one
combat engineer, who had fortified the building. They had 3 light
machine guns, pistols, grenades, and an anti‑tank rifle.

In the morning, Germans assaulted repeatedly from two directions. In the
late afternoon the Germans brought in 75mm artillery and 105mm
howitzers, blasting the building. Finally, they pumped gasoline into the
basement and ignite it. The defenders, burned and suffocating,
surrendered and the 38 survivors were captured.

A German court martial declared them illegal combatants and sentenced
them to death on 5 October 1939. All were executed. The verdict is now
officially recognized as judicial murder.

## Soviet Invasion

The Soviet invasion of Poland began on 17 September 1939, when the Red
Army crossed the eastern Polish border without a declaration of war. The
campaign lasted until 6 October 1939, ending with the partition of
Poland between Nazi Germany and the USSR.

### Occupation

### Germans

Nazi rule in Gdansk was characterized by systematic repression of Poles
and Jews. Since the Nazis had been in power since 1936, they had their
lists well established once Gdansk Fell. There were Mass expulsions
(1939–1943) with between 120,000 and 170,000 Poles and Jews were
deported from Gdansk and nearly 120,000 German settlers were brought in
to replace expelled Polish residents. Between 30,000 and 40,000 members
of the Polish intelligentsia were murdered in Greater Gdansk region as
part of a targeted extermination campaign. Most of the Jews had
previously been expelled so the remaining 6,000 were executed.

### Soviets

After annexation, the Soviet regime began mass repression capturing
320,000–450,000 Polish soldiers, deporting hundreds of thousands of
civilians to Siberia and Kazakhstan in four waves (1939–1941). Secret
Police arrests, executions, and political terror, targeting officers,
police, clergy, and officials were conducted on a regular basis. The
also ran sham elections staged to legitimize annexation.

## Rebirth

As we often do, we went on a walking tour of the city to get our
bearings and learn some of the history. I asked the guide, since Poland
was under Communist rule after the war, how did the magnificent
buildings get so faithfully reproduced. He told me they were Polish
Communists, proud of their heritage and determined to wipe out all
Germanic influences.

After the war, the ethnic German population was removed and replaced by
Polish refugees. There was significant pressure to build simple,
Soviet-style concrete blocks, with some Polish officials arguing against
rebuilding "monuments to Teutonic Invaders."

However, in 1948, a compromise was reached, Architects used 17th and
18th-century maps to reconstruct the city. They intentionally selected
the more Dutch and Flemish-style designs for facades while completely
ignoring the later German-style modifications of the 19th century. Make
1793 (the end of the Polish rule) great again.

Due to these stylistic choices, modern Gdansk appears more "Dutch" today
than it did immediately preceding the war. While the interiors of the
buildings are often communal living blocks, the exterior facades
maintain the historic northern Renaissance aesthetic. If you didn’t know
the history, you would think Gdansk was your standard 1000-year-old
town.

# The Museum of the Second World War

Gdansk’s excellent Museum of the Second World War was open in 2017. The
unique building design has the main exhibition space 3 floors
underground. The building’s dramatic, tilted, rising form and its
division into underground and above‑ground zones symbolize the past
buried beneath, the present around us, and the future rising upward.

<img
src="images/gdansk---the-first-shots-of-the-second-world-war/image_42.jpeg"
style="width:6.5in;height:4.39931in" />

Through Rachel’s research we got a tour with one of the museum curators,
the best way to see the space. His first question was there actually a
second world war was disconcerting, but he was making the point that
many people feel WW2 was just a continuation of WW1 and that each
country defines the start of the war separately. China considers the war
started in 1937 when the Japanese invaded, Czechoslovakia in 1938, most
of the rest of us in 1939 and the US in 1941.

The museum focused more on the impact of civilians than the individual
battles. There was an excellent section of Soviet propaganda, showing
the soldier guiding the people to their true home.

# Lech Walesa – Solidarity Movement

The other key event in Gdansk was the formation of the Solidarity
Movement that led Poland to embrace democracy and was a key factor in
the freedom of many neighbouring countries.

While working at the Lenin Shipyard (now Gdansk Shipyard), Wałęsa, an
electrician, became a trade-union activist, for which he was persecuted
by the government, placed under surveillance, fired in 1976 and arrested
several times. In August 1980, he was instrumental in political
negotiations that led to the ground-breaking Gdansk Agreement between
striking workers and the government. He co-founded the Solidarity trade
union, whose membership rose to more than ten million.

After martial law in Poland was imposed and Solidarity was outlawed,
Wałęsa was arrested again. Released from custody, he continued his
activism and was prominent in the establishment of the Round Table
Agreement that led to the semi-free 1989 Polish parliamentary election
and a Solidarity-led government. He presided over Poland's transition
from Marxist–Leninist state socialism into a market-based liberal
democracy for 6 years, winning the Nobel Peace Prize.

# Final Thoughts

Gdansk was a remarkable city, filled with magnificent buildings and a
rich history. I was fascinated with the rebuilding process and how a
country under communist rule was able to recreate the city so
faithfully. It was evident that Polish Pride ran much deeper than their
politics. I’ve spent a great deal of time in Ypres and Arras, two cities
that faithfully recreated their city centres after World War One. In
those cases, the city inhabitants returned determined to rebuild their
lives. In the case of Gdansk, by the start of the war, the population
was 90% German and those that remained were deported after the war, so
Gdansk was an empty city and the Poles who moved in afterwards did not
have ties to the city’s history.

I’d highly recommend a visit. Our only regret was that a long weekend
was not nearly long enough to explore the rich culture and history of
this vibrant city.

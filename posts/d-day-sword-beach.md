# Introduction

During my previous trips to Normandy, I had visited all of the D-Day
beaches except Sword Beach, and I wanted to correct that oversight.
Sword is the easternmost beach and probably the least explored. The US
beaches of Omaha and Utah have plenty of visitors because of the US
Marketing Machine, Gold Beach has the Mulberry Harbour and Juno Beach is
the Canadian Landing zone. A ferry from the UK lands at Ouistreham, but
most British Visitors usually head straight past Sword Beach to the more
famous sites.

# The Landings

## Overview

Although the Sword Beach Landing area is 5 miles wide, offshore reefs
and the heavily defended port limited the landing area to a one-mile
section. The beaches were labeled from West to East, Peter, Queen and
Roger. The landings took place on Queen White and Queen Red sectors,
forcing the troops to spread out once they made their way inland.

The British 3<sup>rd</sup> Division, supported by the 1<sup>st</sup>
Special Service Brigade (Commandos) landed on Sword. Due to the narrow
beach area, only one Brigade landed at a time as opposed to the two
Brigade landings at the other beaches. The next map shows the conveyor
belt landings over the narrow strip of beach. The landings were tightly
timed, and the rapidly advancing tide quickly limited the beach area
leading to a great deal of congestion. The landings occurred just before
low tide so there was more than 300 m of open beach to cross. At high
tide at 1430 there was only about 30 m of beach.

<img src="images/d-day-sword-beach/image_2.png"
style="width:6.5in;height:8.23958in" />

At 0530 the Naval Bombardment started. The previous Air campaign had
limited impact due to cloud cover, and the intense Naval Bombardment
didn’t take out the reinforced concrete casemates. At 0725 the Duplex
Drive (DD) Tanks from the 13/18 Hussars swam in from about 5,000 yards.
Of the 34 tanks that swam in, 31 made it to the beach. This was much
more successful than those at Omaha beach, of the 29 DD Tanks launched
from 6,000 yards only 5 made it to the beach due to heavy surf
conditions. At the same time, the Armoured Vehicle Royal Artillery
(AVRE) speciality tanks were delivered directly to the beach by the
Landing Craft Tank (LCT) ships. The Petard tanks took out casemates, the
Flail tanks cleared paths through the minefields, and the Crocodile
tanks provided flamethrowers to take out the defenders. More on the
tanks in a bit.

<img src="images/d-day-sword-beach/image_3.png"
style="width:6.5in;height:6.44306in" />

The tanks were slightly delayed, so they landed at the same time as the
initial assault forces of the 2<sup>nd</sup> East Yorkshires and
1<sup>st</sup> South Lancashire Regiment. Beach clearance units started
to clear paths through the beach obstacles, but the rapidly rising tide
hampered their efforts.

Next up were the self-propelled guns of the artillery, follow-on
infantry companies and an hour later the Commandos of \#4 Commandos
along with 177 members of the Free French Commandos who landed on the
eastern edge of the beach. The French Commandos took out the Casino at
Ouistreham and the 1<sup>st</sup> Special Service Brigade under
Brigadier the Lord Lovat who was scheduled to support the paratroopers
who had captured Pegasus Bridge shortly after midnight. Lord Lovat was a
Scot, and he had his piper Bill Millin pipe the troops off the landing
craft. Lord Lovat is on the right just off the ramp and Piper Millen is
getting ready to enter the water.
<img src="images/d-day-sword-beach/image_4.png"
style="width:6.5in;height:6.37778in" />

On the western edge of the invading force, the \#41 Royal Marine
Commandos headed toward Juno Beach to link up with the Canadians.

By 0900 the exits off the beach were cleared and the assault battalions
moved inland.

## Counterattack

The German Armour was held back from immediate counterattacks because
Hitler believed this was only a diversionary attack and that the main
attack would be at Pas de Calais. By late morning the 21 Panzer Division
launched an attack, exploiting the gap between the Canadians at Juno
Beach and the Sword Beach landings.

<img src="images/d-day-sword-beach/image_5.png"
style="width:6.5in;height:3.78264in" />

The Panzers made it to the coast as shown by the pink line, but heavy
naval bombardment and relentless bombing by the RAF Typhoons destroyed
many vehicles and forced their withdrawal.

## Strong Points

Sword Beach was heavily defended with both strong points and other
substantial defences inland as described in a [previous
post](https://www.paulsbattlefieldtours.com/post/normandy-defensive-installations).
The main landings went in between WN20 and WN18. WN stands for
*Widerstandsnest*, a German military term that translates directly to
"resistance nest". These were heavily fortified strongpoints, typically
consisting of concrete bunkers, trench networks, machine gun *tobruks*,
and anti-tank guns. Manned by small garrisons of 20 to 40 soldiers,
these nests were strategically placed to dominate fields of fire. The
Strong Points (SP) or Stützpunkt, was a much larger, more heavily armed,
and complex defensive node. An SP was created by grouping multiple
smaller *WN* together under a single command. These larger complexes
housed more troops, heavy artillery, and multiple interconnected
bunkers. Several of the maps in this post are using the mapping tool
created by Colin McGarry, a long-time Normandy guide.

<img src="images/d-day-sword-beach/image_6.png"
style="width:6.5in;height:4.39444in" />

In addition to the WN and SP, there were houses all along the
waterfront, most with stone walls, which had also fortified and
interconnected with an extensive trench system.

WN20 (codename COD) had an 88mm and several 50mm guns. The initial wave
landed almost directly in front of the WN resulting in heavy infantry
and armoured casualties. The WN was not fully taken until 1000.

WN18 (codename Skate) had a 75mm and a 50mm gun and was responsible for
taking out at least 10 tanks. The WN was not cleared until after 0930.
There is generally not much evidence of the defensive positions on Sword
Beach, but one of the casemates from WN18 remains and is integrated into
a private residence. The gun port, previously used to fire down the
beach is now a window.

## Le Grand Bunker

Almost at the port of Ouistreham is the Le Grand Bunker, a 5-story
observation bunker that has been turned into a museum. There were
anti-aircraft guns on the roof, and a fifth-floor observation window. On
other beach areas there are cliffs, but the flat land here forced the
creation of a tall bunker.

<img src="images/d-day-sword-beach/image_15.jpeg"
style="width:6.5in;height:8.63264in" />

The bunker was taken intact late in the day on D-Day. Once the main door
was breached, the Germans surrendered. The museum is on all 5 floors and
shows the living quarters, communication areas and of course the
observation deck. View from the observation slit.

<img src="images/d-day-sword-beach/image_16.jpeg"
style="width:6.5in;height:4.89444in" />

The optical range finder is the highlight of the observation post.

<img src="images/d-day-sword-beach/image_17.png"
style="width:6.5in;height:3.86042in" />

The optics of the rangefinder are very interesting. The operator looks
through the eyepieces and adjusts the knobs until the images coincide
and read the range off the scale. A 2m rangefinder would have an
accuracy of 100 m at 10 km.

For comparison, the German battleship, the Bismarck, had a 10m
rangefinder which had an accuracy of 20m at 10km, allowing the ship to
have first shot kill capability. A most impressive optical instrument. A
model of the integrated Radar/Rangefinder with a sailor as a reference.

<img src="images/d-day-sword-beach/image_18.jpg"
style="width:5.27083in;height:3.45833in" />

### Museum

There are a number of interesting artifacts outside the museum.

This landing craft was used in the filming of Saving Private Ryan and
when the son (Jonathan) of Bob Orrell, who captured Le Grand Bunker in
1944, heard the boat was going to be destroyed he organized the salvage
and shipment to the museum.

<img src="images/d-day-sword-beach/image_19.jpeg"
style="width:6.5in;height:8.63264in" />

The 88mm FLAK-36 was designed as an anti-aircraft gun but was found to
be so effective against armour that it was used extensively to create
carnage for the Allied Armour in Normandy and beyond. The gun could fire
12-15 rounds per minute at ranges up to 16,000 yards. The same gun was
later mounted on the Tiger I and II tanks.

<img src="images/d-day-sword-beach/image_20.jpeg"
style="width:6.5in;height:8.63264in" />

The M7 Priest 105mm Self-Propelled Artillery gun. At 0730 on D-Day the
LCT of the 58<sup>th</sup> Armoured Field Artillery was hit by a shell
on the way to Omaha Beach and sank. There were 4 Priests on board, and
this one was retrieved in 1982. The Priests were also used by the
Canadian Artillery and my buddy Jim Pickell’s dad landed on Juno Beach
in a similar vehicle.

# Armour on the Beach

## DD Tanks

The Duplex Drive (DD) Sherman Tanks were used to ensure that some tanks
made it ashore. There were concerns that bringing all of the armour
directly to the beach on Landing Craft Tanks (LCT) was dangerous because
if an LCT was hit 6 tanks would be lost at once. The DD tanks were a
regular 30-ton Sherman with an inflatable screen to provide buoyancy,
and two steerable propellers on the back to propel the tank through the
water.

<img src="images/d-day-sword-beach/image_27.png"
style="width:6.5in;height:3.65in" />

Demonstrating remarkable faith in Archimedes and his principles, the
tank inflated the screen, drove off the LCT then headed to shore. The
driver was inside and the rest of the crew were outside just in case
Archimedes had made it all up, the screen tore, or a wave swamped the
tank. The tanks moved about four miles per hour and had about 3 feet of
clearance in calm seas.

<img src="images/d-day-sword-beach/image_28.png"
style="width:6.23533in;height:6.1659in" />

It was anything but calm, so the ships released the tanks around 4,000
yards from shore rather than the prescribed 6,000 yards. Only 3 tanks
sank on the way in, a remarkable achievement. Once the tanks landed it
was a different story as most of the initial wave were destroyed before
making it off the beach. This shows the screen fully inflated, and the
two propellers in place. The propellers were driven from a track cog, so
the tracks were turning the whole time the tank was in the water.

<img src="images/d-day-sword-beach/image_29.png"
style="width:6.5in;height:4.95625in" />

Once the tank made the beach, they would deflate the skirt and
immediately start firing. A DD Tank inland supporting the infantry. Note
the skirt in the deflated position.

<img src="images/d-day-sword-beach/image_30.png"
style="width:6.5in;height:4.75278in" />

## Flail Tanks

Another unique tank variant that was critical for clearing a path
through the mine fields was the Flail tank. Again, it was a standard
Sherman tank with a series of spinning chains that set off anti-tank
mines to clear a path off of the beach. They usually worked in tandem,
like snowplows on a four-lane highway, beating a path two tanks wide at
a one mile per hour clip. It must have been exciting to operate.

<img src="images/d-day-sword-beach/image_31.jpeg"
style="width:6.5in;height:4.14444in" />

## Armoured Vehicle Royal Engineers (AVRE)

### Carpet Layer

The AVRE Tanks were specialist tanks built on the British Churchill
chassis. The carpet layer tank had a giant bobbin and laid carpet to
allow vehicles to cross soft beach sand.

<img src="images/d-day-sword-beach/image_32.png"
style="width:6.5in;height:6.48403in" />

The tradition remains on Sword Beach, but now it is used to get
strollers, wagons and wheelchairs down to the shore.

<img src="images/d-day-sword-beach/image_33.jpeg"
style="width:6.5in;height:8.63264in" />

### Fascines

The AVRE tanks also often carried Fascines, large bundles of wood that
were dumped into an anti-tank ditch to allow the vehicle to cross.

<img src="images/d-day-sword-beach/image_34.png"
style="width:6.5in;height:4.11111in" />

### Crocodile

The Crocodile tank was a flame thrower that could project its flame more
than 150 yards and was very useful for convincing troops to abandon
their bunkers.

<img src="images/d-day-sword-beach/image_35.png"
style="width:6.5in;height:5.86042in" />

### Petard Spigot Mortar

The Petard Spigot Mortar AVRE Tank is the one most often mounted as a
memorial. The 240 mm mortar, nicknamed the flying dustbin, launched a
40-pound demolition charge a maximum of 80 yards and was used to blast
apart sea walls, concrete bunkers or steel obstacles. The tank was
heavily armoured as it had to get very close to its target to be
effective.

<img src="images/d-day-sword-beach/image_36.jpeg"
style="width:6.5in;height:4.89444in" />

The round had a small propelling charge and a large warhead.

<img src="images/d-day-sword-beach/image_37.png"
style="width:6.5in;height:7.12292in" />

This is a fascinating weapon. because it was a spigot mortar, it
couldn't be reloaded from inside the turret. The co-driver had to slide
open a special hatch in the hull floor, break open the mortar by tilting
the barrel up, and slide a new round onto the spigot from the inside,
tilt the barrel back down, ready to fire. This method left them
momentarily vulnerable but was vastly safer than loading from the
outside.

<img src="images/d-day-sword-beach/image_38.jpeg"
style="width:6.5in;height:3.325in" />

### French Commandos

There were 177 French Commandos who landed with the British Commandos on
D-Day. There are multiple memorials to the only French soldiers who
participated in the landings. The major success for the Commandos was to
capture the Casino that had been turned into a strong point. One of the

# Final Thoughts

Each of the landing beaches has a different topography and difficulties
clearing the beach. Sword Beach is considered by some to be a failure as
the troops did not meet their objective of capturing Caen on the first
day and the Allies spent the next month before actually taking the city.
In my opinion, it was hopelessly optimistic to think the troops could
reach Caen as the defenses were severely under-estimated. The 21 Panzer
counterattack also slowed progress, and the hardened structures inland
slowed the British progress.

Almost 29,000 soldiers and 630 tanks landed on Sword Beach on the first
day suffering a relatively light 630 casualties. The Commandos made it
to Pegasus Bridge, relieving the beleaguered Paratroopers and
maintaining control of those critical bridges.

After storming Sword Beach, it was only appropriate that I celebrate the
accomplishment with some awesome Moules et Frites.
